Two shortcuts you already own: Win plus period for symbols, and Win plus V for your clipboard history

Windows 11 has been keeping a symbol drawer and a clipboard history behind two keys most people never press.

How-To Geek's Zachary Boddy makes the case for Win + . (period), the Emoji Picker shortcut, and points out that the panel holds far more than emoji and GIFs: currencies from around the world, mathematical symbols for formulas, exponents and fractions, unique letters and accents from several languages, plus ASCII emoticons and kaomoji, which is the end of searching the web for a character or memorizing an Alt code. The same panel carries Windows clipboard history, and Win + V opens straight to it, so recently copied text, links, and images are one keystroke away instead of a window switch away. It remembers the items you reach for most, lets you pin the ones you always need, and clears the whole history in a single click. Boddy notes that once you enable it in settings, that clipboard history can also sync across your Windows 11 devices, either automatically or manually if you would rather approve each one.

The tool shelf new tonight

TeraCopy

Free; Pro license $49.95Windows, with a Mac versionHow-To Geek, Aug 30

TeraCopy takes over the copy and move jobs Windows Explorer normally handles, and How-To Geek's Jorge A. Aguilar keeps it on every new PC for four reasons: a file that will not copy gets logged and skipped instead of freezing the whole queue, a second verification pass hashes what landed on disk and flags a mismatch rather than failing quietly, a transfer can be paused and later resumed from the exact byte offset where it stopped, and it hooks into drag and drop, Ctrl+C, and Ctrl+V so nothing about your habits changes; Code Sector's own page is where the limits are, since the free license covers verification, timestamps, locked files, checksum files, secure delete, and up to two copy threads, while multiple destinations, more threads, editable file lists, favorite folders, HTML and CSV reports, and commercial use belong to the $49.95 Pro license, and Aguilar is honest that if you only ever move a few small documents you do not need any of this.

Google AI Edge Gallery

Free, Apache 2.0Android 12+, iOS 17+, macOSXDA Developers, Aug 30

Google AI Edge Gallery downloads open models onto the phone itself and runs them there with no internet connection, offering chat with a Thinking Mode that shows the reasoning steps, image analysis, audio transcription and translation, a Prompt Lab for testing, and Agent Skills that reach the calendar, reminders, maps, and phone controls such as the flashlight and volume, and it installs from Google Play, the App Store, or a direct APK in the GitHub releases for anyone without a store; the caveats are real, since XDA's Nolen Jonker found no wake word and no smart home control, the Agent Skills need the Gemma 4 models specifically, and the context window is held near 32K tokens by the RAM a phone actually has.

Hyperfocus

Free forever, no paid planmacOS app, local-firstProduct Hunt No. 2, Aug 30

Hyperfocus is a planner that starts from goals rather than an inbox, walking you through flagging the few outcomes that matter, planning a weekly cycle around them, organizing the day, and then working in time-boxed focus blocks, and its own site states plainly that it is free forever with no Pro subscription, no paid plan, and no pack of AI credits to buy; the honest caveat is reach, because it is currently available only for macOS with no Windows, iPhone, or Android version, and while the planner itself is local-first and works offline, the optional AI feedback on your goals runs through third-party providers such as OpenRouter, Google, and OpenAI, free for a 14-day trial and paid directly to those providers after that.

Every shelf item is available today; no waitlists, no vaporware.

Five Google Maps features for a busy day, from a nine stop route to a parking pin that saves itself

About 10 minutesGoogle Maps on a phoneHow-To Geek front page, Aug 30

Plan the whole errand run How-To Geek's T.M. Amrita searches the first destination, taps Directions, then uses Add stop to chain on up to nine more and drags them into a sensible order with the two-line handle, which works for driving, walking, and cycling but not public transit; she saves places into custom lists from the bookmark icon under You, turns on Automatically save parking under Settings, Navigation, Parking options so a crowded lot stops being a memory test, checks Street View from the layers icon before visiting somewhere unfamiliar since blue-highlighted roads are the ones with coverage, and taps the trip panel mid-drive to Share trip progress so whoever is waiting at the airport can see the arrival time instead of texting for it.

One standing instruction that tells Claude to assume and keep moving instead of stalling on questions

Under 5 minutesA Claude accountMakeUseOf front page, Aug 30

Assume, then say so MakeUseOf's Saikat Basu adds one behavioral rule rather than a longer prompt, telling the assistant to answer directly and move the work forward, to make a reasonable assumption and briefly state it when something is missing, and to ask a follow-up only when the missing piece is genuinely necessary; he shows the three places it can live, in Claude's Settings under Profile Preferences for every chat, in Project Instructions for one kind of work, or pasted at the top of a single prompt, and he is careful to say this is not a blanket ban on questions, recommending the conditional version and steady refinement based on which assumptions it gets wrong.

Trust your phone as a level and a compass, and keep real hardware for everything you have to measure

About 15 minutesAn Android phone with a gyroscopeAndroid Police, Aug 30

Two sensors worth trusting Android Police's Marvin Velasco tested four to five free apps in each category on a single ARCore-compatible Android phone and found the honest split: a bubble level running off the gyroscope and accelerometer worked perfectly fine and could be calibrated, and a magnetometer-based digital compass pointed true without any calibration at all, while AR measuring apps came in off by an inch or two with inconsistent repeats, a light meter was awkward to position, and a guitar tuner lagged and missed; the thermometer and elevation apps were the clear failures, since a phone has no temperature sensor to read and the altitude figure simply came back wrong.
