Stirling PDF brings more than 60 PDF tools to your own computer, free for individual use

The PDF chores nobody enjoys, merging, splitting, compressing, signing, can all be done tonight without handing the file to a website you have never heard of.

Stirling PDF is an open-source PDF workbench with more than 60 tools for reading, editing, signing, redacting, converting, and automating documents, and its own site says the editor runs on your machine, is free for individual use, and has passed 30 million downloads. Its GitHub repository carries 90,700 stars and 8,200 forks and calls itself the number one PDF application on GitHub. XDA's August 27 write-up describes the version 2.0 interface, a Multi-Tool Workspace that chains several edits without re-uploading the file, and a Mobile Scanner that hands a phone photo to the desktop app over a QR code. The honest limit is that a few features still want server-side processing, and those prompt you to connect to Stirling Cloud or a server you run yourself.

The tool shelf new tonight

CoMaps

FreeAndroid, iOS, Mac, LinuxHacker News, 327 points

CoMaps is a community-run fork of Organic Maps and Maps.me that searches, routes, and speaks turn-by-turn directions with no mobile data, keeps hiking trails and offline Wikipedia articles on the phone, states plainly that it does not identify you, track you, or collect information, and is free for everyone through Google Play, F-Droid, the iOS App Store, the Mac App Store, and Flathub; the caveat is coverage, since its public transit layer reaches only a growing list of cities, so check your own before you rely on it.

Lightworks

Free tier, paid tiers aboveWindows, Mac, Linux desktopXDA Developers, Aug 27

Lightworks is a long-running video editor whose free tier, listed at zero on the company's own pricing page, includes timeline editing, automatic saving, advanced transitions, and simple visual effects on Windows, macOS, and Linux, and XDA's Nolen Jonker says it replaced his Premiere Pro subscription outright, with no watermark, an installer under 100MB, proxy files for modest hardware, and background rendering while you work; the catch is stated on that same pricing page, since free exports stop at 720p and some Linux functionality is restricted.

Every shelf item is available today; no waitlists, no vaporware.

The workflows follow along at home

Set your 2.4GHz Wi-Fi to channel 1, 6, or 11 by hand and stop it overlapping the neighbors

15 minutesRouter admin page accessMakeUseOf front page, Aug 27

Three channels, no overlap Gavin Phillips explains that the 2.4GHz band holds 83.5MHz of usable spectrum cut into 11 channels spaced 5MHz apart while each channel is about 22MHz wide, so only 1, 6, and 11 avoid stepping on each other: run a Wi-Fi analyzer in the evening when the building is busiest, pick the quietest of the three in your router's settings instead of leaving it on automatic, move whatever devices you can to 5GHz or 6GHz, and rescan now and then, because in a dense area you can still end up sharing a channel.

Six Windows 11 habits that give back a few minutes a day, starting with one update command

20 minutesWindows 11, Pro for SandboxMakeUseOf front page, Aug 27

Six switches and shortcuts Pankil Shah updates every Winget-installed program at once by running winget upgrade -all in Command Prompt, turns on Settings, System, Advanced, End task so a frozen window can be closed straight from the taskbar, opens unknown software inside Windows Sandbox where everything is deleted on exit, mirrors an Android screen through Phone Link, remaps three and four finger touchpad gestures, and keeps Win+V clipboard history and Win+. emoji within reach; two limits are worth knowing, since Sandbox needs Windows 11 Pro and the Winget command leaves Microsoft Store apps alone.

Read the Google Maps traffic layer before you pull out of the driveway, not just the ETA

1 minuteGoogle Maps on a phoneXDA front page, Aug 27

Look before you drive Parth Shah taps the map icon on the Google Maps home screen, chooses Traffic under Map details, and reads the color-coded roads around the trip, spotting the red and dark red stretches before navigation begins rather than trusting a single estimated arrival time, then taps the three dot menu after entering directions to set a departure or arrival time and see how the drive should look later; he is honest that the habit pays best on routes you already know, because seeing the jam only helps if you know the way around it.
