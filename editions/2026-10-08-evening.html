KDE Connect links your phone to your computer for files, clipboard and notifications, free on every platform

One free install on each device, and the gap between your phone and your desk mostly closes.

KDE Connect pairs a phone with a computer over the local network and then carries the small things between them: files and links, a shared clipboard, phone notifications shown on the desktop, music controls, and the phone itself as a virtual trackpad or a presentation remote. KDE publishes it for Android through Google Play, F-Droid and the Huawei AppGallery, for Windows through the Microsoft Store, Chocolatey or a standalone installer, for macOS by direct download or Homebrew, for iOS through the App Store, and for every major Linux distribution and desktop, and the project's own page states that it is open source and free to use for any purpose forever. It will also ring a phone you have misplaced, report its battery level on your desktop, and push a link out of the Firefox or Chrome context menu. The working assumption is that both devices sit on the same network: KDE points to OpenVPN support for public Wi-Fi that will not let devices see each other, and the iOS build ships with a README of data disclosures and known limitations.

KDE Connect

Free and open sourceAndroid, iOS and every desktopAndroid Police, Oct 8

Moves files, links, clipboard text, notifications and media controls between a phone and a computer, with builds in Google Play, F-Droid, the Microsoft Store, Homebrew and the App Store, and the honest limit is the network: both devices are expected to be on the same one, with OpenVPN offered for public Wi-Fi that blocks them from seeing each other, and the iOS version carries its own README of data disclosures and known limitations.

Nilesoft Shell

Free and open sourceWindows 7 to 11, portableMakeUseOf, Oct 8

Rebuilds the File Explorer right-click menu so you can hide the entries software has added over the years and put your own commands, sub-menus and icons there instead, running portable with no installation on Windows 7 through 11, and the catch is the configuration: every setting lives in a plain text file called shell.nss, which MakeUseOf's Amir Bohlooli describes as editing code with no graphical interface and no drag-and-drop.

X-plore File Manager

Free, donate for extrasAndroid, dual-pane file managerMakeUseOf, Oct 8

Shows two folder trees side by side on an Android phone so a copy has a visible source and destination, and reaches Google Drive, OneDrive, Dropbox, Box and WebDAV alongside LAN, FTP, SSH and DLNA shares, but the useful extras are gated: the developer puts Wi-Fi sharing, SSH, the disk map and the encrypted Vault behind an in-app donation, and MakeUseOf's Tashreef Shareef reports full-page ads in the free version, on opening the app and while moving between folders, and calls the interface not the prettiest.

Every shelf item is available today; no waitlists, no vaporware.

The workflows follow along at home

Your router may still be set to a security mode from 2003, and it caps the whole house at 54Mbps

About fifteen minutesYour router's admin pageMakeUseOf front, Oct 8

Open the router admin page MakeUseOf's Tashreef Shareef signs in at 192.168.0.1 or 192.168.1.1, opens the wireless security settings and moves the mode off WPA with TKIP, a protocol introduced in 2003 that caps a connection at 54Mbps and leaves roughly 20 to 25Mbps once overhead is paid, onto WPA2-AES or WPA3; after saving, reconnecting the devices and running a speed test, a 100Mbps line at their parents' house went from about 22Mbps to roughly 85Mbps, and the write-up names the WMM setting as the other switch worth checking when the numbers still look wrong.

Three free storage tiers and a cleanup habit replace the cloud subscription you keep renewing

An hour, then monthlyFree accounts on two or three servicesAndroid Police front, Oct 8

Spread the files, not the money Android Police's Anu Joy keeps documents and mail inside Google's shared 15GB, parks the PC files in OneDrive's 5GB and sends photos to Amazon Photos, which holds photos without a limit for Prime members alongside 5GB for video and other files, then names MEGA's encrypted 20GB, Proton Drive's 5GB and pCloud's up-to-10GB as the next pools to add; the honest part is that it only holds together with maintenance, emptying trash, sorting by file size and watching each service's usage, so the subscription is paid off in habit rather than cash.

Quick Share sends a file from Android to a Windows PC or a supported iPhone with no internet

Under five minutesBluetooth on both devicesMakeUseOf front, Oct 8

Share, then pick the device MakeUseOf's Varun Krishan long-presses a file, taps Share, chooses Quick Share and waits for the other device to accept, with Bluetooth handling the discovery before a direct Wi-Fi link carries the transfer and mobile data waiting as a fallback if that link drops; the receiving device needs Bluetooth on and its screen unlocked, a QR code covers anyone who is not in your contacts, two devices on the same Google Account can accept automatically, and the feature now reaches Windows PCs through a downloadable app and AirDrop-compatible iPhones and iPads as well as other Android phones.

Six Android shortcuts put the hotspot, flashlight, camera and a screenshot one gesture away

About ten minutesAn Android phoneMakeUseOf front, Oct 8

Edit the quick tiles first MakeUseOf's Brandon Miniman adds a Hotspot tile from the notification shade's edit screen under Connectivity, puts the flashlight on the lock screen through Settings, Display and Touch, Lock screen, Shortcuts, sets a double press of the power button to open the camera or Google Wallet under Settings, System, Gestures, swipes down with two fingers to land straight on the quick tiles, switches on Quick Tap so a tap on the back of the phone takes a screenshot one-handed, and maps a long press of the power button to the digital assistant; the one thing to expect is that the menu names move around, since a Samsung phone hides the lock screen shortcut under Wallpaper and style instead.
